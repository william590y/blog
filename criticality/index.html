How to read the plot

The horizontal axis is \(E\), the fraction of excitatory neurons that are active. The vertical axis is \(I\), the same for inhibitory neurons. A single point is the state of the whole population. The model gives every point a velocity, and the tracers are carried along by it, so their streaks trace out the flow:

\[\dot E = -\alpha E + (1-E)\,\Phi\!\left(s^E\right),\qquad \dot I = -\alpha I + (1-I)\,\Phi\!\left(s^I\right)\]
\[s^E = w_{EE}E - w_{EI}I + h,\qquad s^I = w_{IE}E - w_{II}I + h,\qquad \Phi(s)=\begin{cases}\tanh s, & s>0\\[2pt] 0, & s\le 0\end{cases}\]

Each equation balances losses against gains. Active units switch off at rate \(\alpha\), which gives the \(-\alpha E\) term. The inactive fraction \(1-E\) switches on at rate \(\Phi(s^E)\), where \(s^E\) is the net input: excitation from \(E\) minus inhibition from \(I\), plus any external drive \(h\). These are the large-network, fully connected limit of the stochastic binary-neuron model that the paper actually simulates.

Why the plane splits into three regions

\(\Phi\) has a kink at zero: it follows \(\tanh\) for positive input and is exactly zero for negative input. So the right-hand side changes formula on the two lines \(s^E=0\) and \(s^I=0\), which the paper calls switching manifolds. With \(h=0\) both pass through the origin, along \(I=(w_{EE}/w_{EI})\,E\) and \(I=(w_{IE}/w_{II})\,E\). For the Fig. 1 weights those slopes are \(5/3\) and \(3\).

I\(s^E>0,\ s^I>0\)the full nonlinear systemThe only place an active fixed point can exist.

III\(s^E\le 0,\ s^I\le 0\)\(\dot E=-\alpha E,\ \dot I=-\alpha I\)Both decay at the same rate, straight into the origin.

Why regions II and III are traps

In region III both variables shrink by the same factor \(e^{-\alpha t}\), so the ratio \(I/E\) never changes. Every trajectory there is a straight ray into the origin, which you can see in the grey tracer streaks. It cannot leave, because region III is defined by \(I/E\) being large enough.

For region II, follow the excitatory input along a trajectory, \(\dot s^E = w_{EE}\dot E - w_{EI}\dot I\). On the line \(s^E=0\) we have \(w_{EE}E=w_{EI}I\) and \(\Phi(s^E)=0\), and substituting both equations gives the same answer from either side:

\[\left.\frac{d s^E}{dt}\right|_{s^E=0} = -\,w_{EI}\,(1-I)\,\tanh\!\left(s^I\right) \;<\; 0 .\]

So the excitatory input is always falling where the state meets the line. Trajectories cross from region I into region II, never the other way. This is the crossing (rather than sliding) result of the paper's Appendix C. Launch a path near the cyan line in the Fig. 1 preset and watch its time series change shading.

Quiescent or active: the origin decides

With \(h=0\), \(E=I=0\) is always a fixed point: the quiescent, or absorbing, state. Kicks into regions II and III die out no matter what. Kicks into region I see the linearization

\[J=\begin{pmatrix} w_{EE}-\alpha & -w_{EI}\\ w_{IE} & -(\alpha+w_{II})\end{pmatrix},\qquad \lambda_\pm=\tfrac12\Big[\,w_{EE}-2\alpha-w_{II}\;\pm\;\sqrt{(w_{EE}+w_{II})^2-4w_{EI}w_{IE}}\,\Big].\]

Two thresholds on \(w_{EE}\) come out of this matrix:

  • Transcritical line, where \(\det J=0\): \(\;w_{EE}^{T}=\alpha+\dfrac{w_{EI}w_{IE}}{\alpha+w_{II}}\). Above it the origin is unstable and an active fixed point appears in region I. When this happens continuously it is a directed-percolation transition.
  • Hopf line, where \(\operatorname{tr}J=0\) with complex \(\lambda\): \(\;w_{EE}^{H}=2\alpha+w_{II}\). Past it the linearized flow would spiral outward, but the spiral runs into \(s^E=0\), enters region II and dies. The oscillation is frustrated, and the quiescent state becomes excitable: a small kick can grow into a large excursion before it decays. The Fig. 2b preset shows this.

The paper does not state \(\alpha\) for Fig. 1. Taking \(\alpha=1\), as in Figs. 2–3, the Fig. 1 weights give \(\operatorname{tr}J=0\), \(\det J=0\) and a zero discriminant. Both eigenvalues vanish and \(J\) is nilpotent (Bogdanov–Takens form). The weights satisfy \(w_{IE}=w_{EI}=\alpha+w_{II}\) and \(w_{EE}=2\alpha+w_{II}\), which is exactly the Hopf-tricritical point \(T_5\), the paper's new H+TDP class. That is why decay near the origin is so slow in that preset: it is algebraic, \(E\propto 1/t\), not exponential.

Things to try

  • In the Fig. 1 preset, click just right of the origin, near \((0.05, 0.01)\). The path swings out to about \((0.13, 0.12)\) before it creeps back. That transient growth is the mechanism behind the anomalous spreading exponent \(\eta=2\) at \(T_5\).
  • Raise \(w_{EE}\) above 2.5. A stable active fixed point appears in region I; at \(w_{EE}=3\) it sits near \((0.38, 0.29)\). Starts in region III still die, since the trap argument does not depend on \(w_{EE}\).
  • Load Bistable. The saddle splits the plane: starts on one side reach the active state, the rest decay to quiescence.
  • Load Fig. 2b and watch the path from \((0.3, 0.05)\): the time series is non-monotonic before the collapse in region II.
  • Make \(w_{EE}/w_{EI} > w_{IE}/w_{II}\), for example \(w_{EI}=0.5\). The two lines swap order, and the middle region becomes \(s^E>0,\ s^I<0\), where only inhibition is switched off.
  • Set \(h>0\). The lines no longer meet at the origin, the input there is positive, and the quiescent state is gone.
Velocities are computed from Eqs. (3)–(4) directly; tracers use a midpoint step and trajectories use fourth-order Runge–Kutta with step 0.01. Fixed points are found by Newton's method inside region I.
